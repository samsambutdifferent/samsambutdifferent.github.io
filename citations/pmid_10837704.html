Insulin analogs with improved pharmacokinetic profiles
Brange J, Vølund A.
Advanced Drug Delivery Reviews · 1999 Feb 1;35(2-3):307-335
PMID: 10837704
DOI: 10.1016/S0169-409X(98)00079-9
Abstract

The aim of insulin replacement therapy is to normalize blood glucose in order to reduce the complications of diabetes. The pharmacokinetics of the traditional insulin preparations, however, do not match the profiles of physiological insulin secretion. The introduction of recombinant DNA technology opened new ways to create insulin analogs with altered properties.

Fast-acting analogs are based on the idea that an insulin with less tendency to self-association than human insulin would be more readily absorbed into the systemic circulation after subcutaneous injection. The driving force for self-association in native human insulin resides primarily in the B-chain C-terminus (residues B24–B28), which forms an antiparallel β-sheet at the dimer interface. Point mutations at B28 (ProB28→Asp in insulin aspart) or B26–B28 (HisB10→Asp/ProB28→Lys/LysB29→Pro in insulin lispro) disrupt this interface, yielding predominantly monomeric species that absorb two to three times faster than soluble human insulin.

Protracted-acting analogs have been created to mimic the slow, steady rate of insulin secretion in the fasting state. Two principal strategies have been pursued: isoelectric precipitation at physiological pH (e.g., insulin glargine, engineered by adding two arginine residues to the B-chain C-terminus and substituting GlyA21 for AsnA21) and fatty-acid acylation to promote reversible albumin binding (e.g., insulin detemir, acylated at LysB29 with a C14 myristic acid chain).

The present paper provides a historical review of the efforts to change the physicochemical and pharmacological properties of insulin in order to improve insulin therapy. The available clinical studies of the new insulins are surveyed and show, together with modeling results, that new strategies for optimal basal–bolus treatment are required for utilization of the new fast-acting analogs.
